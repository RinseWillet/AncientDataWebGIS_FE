# Roman Roads in the Nijmegen-Xanten Area

This study provides both an overview and a critical assessment of the study of Roman roads in the eastern part of the Netherlands, mostly the province of Gelderland, and the western part of Germany, specifically the area west of Xanten in Nordrhein Westfalen. The road network was renowned with the capital of Rome being the central node for all roads. The image of perfectly straight paved roads, marked with milestones and side ditches, creating secure lines of mobility and communication, is almost stereotypically perpetuated as a typically Roman feature.

The Rhine traditionally is regarded and researched as the northern continental border of the Roman Empire. Recent scholarship has emphasized the Rhine as a major traffic artery that needed to be controlled by military bases (e.g. Franconi 2014). At the same time, the majority of military installations is found on the left side of the Rhine, which shows that the base of control is situated here, while the military structures on the right side of this river tend to be temporary in nature (e.g. the temporary camps near Ermelo or Flüren). The majority of confirmed sightings of Roman roads are also found on the left side of the Rhine as well. This corresponds well with the conception that the primary function of roads has been assumed to facilitate communication and mobility between military installations. There seems to be a consensus in the scholarship that the Roman road system was constructed by the army, with specific function to facilitate transport in the name of the state (e.g. Hagen 1931; Rathmann 2004). Roads are therefore strongly tied to the mechanisms of the state to consolidate control over an area and facilitate the communication and mobility necessary to sustain this control. In this study these models and interpretations are assessed against the (lack of) archaeological evidence.

Apart from studying the archaeological remains, this study delves into the origins of the Roman Road network. On the one hand the roads constructed under the Roman Empire connecting cities, forts, settlements, temples, and so on, are given special attention in historical and archaeological discourses, as they are presented in many ways as the first precursors or ancestors of modern roads. Furthermore, civil engineering projects and especially roads and the expertise to construct them, can be regarded as central components to the rise of the modern state (and the colonial enterprises of those states; Harvey & Knox 2015, p. 9). With modern preoccupations with past Empires therefore come the preoccupations with past infrastructure. Although hardly any study would celebrate (openly) the civilizing qualities of the Roman Empire, many studies have considered the roads the Empire constructed over its vast territory as a clear innovation and few studies have paid much attention on the possible pre-existing network of routes and roads.

A recent study by a team lead by Carl-Johan Dalgaard is a nice example of this. The study corelated greater Roman road density with greated modern road density, settlement formation in 500 CE and greater economic activity in 2010-2020 CE (Dalgaard et al. 2022). As datasets this study used the roads and settlements as catalogued in Richard Talbert (2000)’s Barrington Atlas of the Greek and Roman World, as well as modern road maps and satellite imagery of light emission at night, the latter being taken as a proxy for economic activity. Although to be celebrated as a thought experiment using (problematic) datasets available, it is clear that this study oversimplifies the development of settlements before (and the 1500 years after) Roman involvement in certain areas and the connectivity that existed. One could easily point to Asia Minor for example, where urban expansion was already taken place well before the Roman  Republic inherited the Kingdom of Attalos III in 133 BCE (Willet 2020). Again, roads, including the famous long distance Royal Road, were established in Asia Minor, although David French did suggest a model for the evolution of roads from the Achaemenid period onwards whereby the Roman Republic formalized the road system and their construction with pavement and milestones (French 1980). That being said, a recent study of a road to the south of Vezirköprü (Neoklaudiopolis) in the north of Asia Minor shows a remarkably long period of use. This road is attested in Hittite itineraries and a Hittite fort is even constructed along the road. During Roman Imperial times, excavation revealed that it had been ‘updated’ to Roman standards (deep roadbed with gravel surface pavement and the addition of milestones) and the road has certainly been attested by Ottoman sources to have been in use well into the 17th century CE (Bekker-Nielsen 2021, pp. 249-266). These examples showcase that the roadsystem or ancient settlement patterns are not necessarily (fully) accountable to the Roman Empire and the suggestion that the Empire planted the seeds for economic development via its infrastructure cannot be sustained. Continuity of the Roman road system from a protohistoric or prehistoric roadsystem has been assumed for the European provinces as well, although rarely studied in depth (e.g. Morriss 2005, p. 25; Vermeulen & Antrop 2001). Therefore, this study wishes to address this by trying to assess to what extent the roads constructed under the Roman Empire in the research area were new and how far they are planted on top of or anchored within an already existing network of routes and roads.
