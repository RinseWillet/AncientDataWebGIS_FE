# Prehistoric Roads, Routes, and Methodologies

In the 1960s, prehistorian Karl Marschalleck lamented the state of research on prehistoric roads and streets: “*Merkwürdigerweise ist die Straßenforschung von prähistorischer, historischer und geographischer Seite bis vor einigen Jahrzehnten völlig vernachlässigt gewesen. Nur mit den Römerstraßen hat man sich eingehender beschäftigt. Der durchschnittliche Prähistoriker älterer Schule klebte am Fundstück. Alles, was über den Aussagewert eines Fundes oder höchstens eines im Gelände sichtbaren Denkmals hinausging, war für ihm uninteressant”* (1964, p. 411). Indeed, by the 1960s, many articles and books in German had been devoted to the topic of Roman roads, while on pre-Roman roads only a few articles had been written (e.g. Hinz 1951; Kersten 1951; Röschmann 1963). Marschalleck continued to set out a reconstruction for prehistoric roads in northern Germany (Schleswig) using a combined approach of toponyms (e.g. *Ochsenweg* or *Königsweg*, p. 416) and historical geography, the position of archaeological sites, especially prehistoric cemeteries and barrows, the morphology of the landscape, and cart tracks and other archaeological traces of roads. This approach is also found in Jakob Röschmann’s study on the prehistory of Flensburg (1963), which focuses on the landscape, the position of settlements and tumuli, and the routes of historically known roads and their toponyms (*Heerweg, Tondernweg, Königsweg, Ochsenweg, Ossenstieg, -pad*; 1963, pp. 101-7). He recognizes that in the network of trafficking routes through the area, the rivers (especially the Treene) must have played an important role, as they provided a source of food and comparatively easy travel through the terrain by following the river levees (pp. 101-2). The rivers and streams also formed attractors for later settlement and later routes of the road network. Although he mostly documents medieval or early modern remains of roads, emphasizing their role as traffic routes for livestock and trade from written sources, their correlation with the position of prehistoric settlements and barrows, river fords, valley crossings etc. leads Röschmann to identify these roads with (prehistoric) roads.

To some extent, this methodology is also found in the road reconstruction of Friedrich Gorissen (1952; 1956) for the area between Kleve and Nijmegen, although his focus is more the road system and its development for medieval and early modern times. Perhaps of greater interest is that the methodology is not fundamentally different as those found in the works on Roman roads of the 19th century. Noteworthy is Jacob Schneider (e.g. 1868), a highschool teacher in mathematics and physics who wrote extensively on Roman roads in the Lower Rhine area on both the right and left bank of the river, based in no small part on own local observations. To a degree, this anchors the methodology for prehistoric road research in a solid and long tradition of research and works like that by Joseph Hagen (1931) validated the approach. Yet fundamentally, it demotes the archaeological object of the road to a secondary position, since it is presumably possible to reconstruct routes of roads and the existence of roads through proxies like toponyms, positions of archaeological sites that are not roads, landscape morphology etc. This must in part be why roads, both for prehistoric, Roman, and even medieval times have received comparatively little fieldwork. Although the use of proxies have become quite advanced with the use of digital analyses (e.g. Network Analysis, Least Cost Path Analysis) the majority of actual (rescue) excavations of roads in the Low Countries have been focused on the *limes* road, especially in the western Netherlands. As these archaeological observations are the means to confirm reconstructions via proxies, biases are bound to arise (this work is far from free of such biases). This, despite the fact that the roads may well constitute the largest sites in existence in The Netherlands and Germany (e.g. the distance between Katwijk and Nijmegen is approximately 100 km. Using a width of, say, 8 m, the *limes* road here would already constitute a site of 80 ha). The (relative) paucity of excavated observations of Roman roads is also visible in the study of prehistoric roads. This section will therefore focus on the used methodologies and results.

It is impossible to summarize the complexities of the movement of people *vis à vis* overland routes, paths and roads in the millenia before the Romans entered the Low Countries. That people have been moving and exchanging goods in North Western Europe long before Romulus and Remus even founded Rome can be learned from a cursorary glance at the literature. Rather, this part will focus on the possible origins of and evidence for prehistoric routes, roads, and paths. Attention is given to the methods for reconstructing prehistoric routes and the problems they have as there is an overlap with the methodologies for the reconstruction of Roman routes and routes.

People have been migrating long before sedentary lifestyles began and in pre-sedentary life, we may assume that seasonal migrations probably mostly followed routes in the landscape that agreeable to transport on foot. Valleys were mostly passed on their narrowest points, rivers and streams were traversed at (seasonal) fords, peatbogs were crossed (if at all) in places which allowed it and so on. These movements of people may have already resulted in paths of some sort, although archaeological evidence for Paleolithic and Mesolithic paths is not existent for the research area. Sake Jager (1985, p. 185) suggests that these seasonal paths form the basis of the network of tracks or roads in Neolithic times. With the arrival of sedentary lifestyles and communities became more ‘fixed’ in the landscape, paths connecting settlements locally and farther away started to play a larger role. On the local level, the farmhouse needed to be connected to agricultural fields or pastures for livestock. Between settlements, exchange of goods as archaeological evidence suggest, must have been facilitated by paths or roads and waterways, either inland or possibly even coastal waters. As most prehistoric (or Roman for that matter) settlements were small with less than 500 inhabitants, it can be concluded that communities must have been in regular contact and intercommunal relationships and/or intermarriages played a vital role in the continued existence of communities. Roads or paths will therefore mostly have consisted of local and short connections between settlements.

Archaeologically, little is known on pre- or protohistoric roads in the research area. For the Netherlands, studies by Sake Jager (1985) and Jan Bakker (1976; 2008) are exemplary for the reconstructions of prehistoric roads in the 20th century. Jager’s reconstruction focuses on a track that connects Eext in the province of Drenthe to a megalithic dolmen nearby (*hunebed* d8). He used three pillars of approaches to reconstruct this track and its biography. It is useful to explore these as they are commonly applied in reconstructions of (pre)historic routes. The first one is historical geography, which uses old maps, toponyms, archives, and oral history / local knowledge as source-material. The logic of this pillar is that tracks and roads have a long sequence of continuity, meaning that the roads mapped on historical maps may have their origins or orientation anchored in (much) older tracks and paths. The morphology of presently existent roads substantiate this to some extent: continued traffic over the same road may cause to road to be hollowed out, or, when the soil was soft, it may cause parallel lanes to develop. This logic is found in reconstructions of Roman roads as well, for example in the 19th century work of Friedrich Schmidt, Jacob Schneider, C.R. Hermans, but also in Joseph Hagen’s (1931) *opus magnum* as well as in Paul van der Heijden’s (2016) reconstructions of Roman roads in the Netherlands.

The second pillar is to use the physical geography of the landscape to reconstruct the landscape. The landscape can roughly be divided first off in areas that are friendly to traffic, and areas that are hostile to traffic, such as marshes, peat bogs, etc. (Jager 1985, p. 186). To some extent, the landscape dictates the possibilities for transport routes, although changes can be made to the landscape to overcome some limitations. Crossing places for peat bogs or marshy areas can be overcome by bridges or causeways for example. To use the physical morphology of the landscape as a determinant in reconstructing routes is found in Bakker’s work as well, who mentions the optimum paths (paths with the least amount of effort needed to move between two points in the landscape) to explain the prehistoric barrow roads in Jutland (Bakker 1976, pp. 66-8). The logic can in no small part be traced to the work of the geographer Peter Haggett (1965), a member of the New Geography school of studying human geography and who produced new approaches and analyses that where picked up especially by prehistorians (see for example Colin Renfrew’s (1969) lauding review) and is to some extent still very much alive with the application of GIS methods like Least Cost Path Analysis or Cost Distance Analysis.

The third pillar is the use of archaeological remains itself or archaeological geography as Jager names this (p. 186). The distribution of settlements, cemeteries and loose small finds are assumed to contain patterns that can be explained by a paths and roads. Roads were reconstructed using the orientation of traces of fences, houses, etc. in settlement excavations, while field boundaries and / or the siting of *raatakkers* can also give insight in ancient trackways. The position of barrows have been used to find (linear) patterns which are then used as proxies for ancient roads (Bakker 1976; 2008; Jager 1985) and other prehistoric cemeteries, such as urnfields, have been used to find linear patterns as well. There are instances where urnfields were found with tracks that were contemporary to or even predating the cemeteries, such as at the urnfields of Sleen and Noord Barge, where a long strip of land containing no graves was found going through the urnfields, indicative of a track or road (Kooi 1979, pp. 152). At the urnfield of Anlo, two groups of graves were situated in straight lines, which led Pieter Kooi to suggest that an intersection of roads existed here (ibid. p. 156). At Gasteren, an urnfield with a 5 m wide gap running northeast-southwest was excavated, suggesting the presence of a road (idem). At Vledder, two parallel rows of graves with a wide strip in between is again suggested by Pieter Kooi to represent a road (ibid. pp. 156-158). Of interest that in all examples, no cart-tracks were found. Kooi suggested that this is not surprising, as the known wheel diameter was c. 80 cm and the excavation plane was situated 30-40 cm below present surface. The remains were simply not deep enough to have cart tracks preserved. In northern Germany (near Meckelstedt in Wesermünde), excavations during the late 1930s revealed a peat bog trackway and dated to the early Bronze Age (Lincke 1939, pp. 125-45; dating based on the construction methods; Schröder 1939, pp. 146-150 for support of the dating based on pollen analysis). The road consisted here of a timber trackway and it lined up with lines of Funnel Beaker funeral monuments and Bronze Age tumuli and a sandy ridge within the peat bog, connecting the sandy rising grounds with a route through the peatbog area. The road could be reconstructed for nearly 2 km. Later historical road of medieval and early modern times followed the same line as the Bronze Age road (Lincke 1939, pp. 140-4). Modderman’s (1955, p. 61) observation on a line of tumuli and other sepulchral prehistoric finds near Epe and his interpretation of this line being indicative for a prehistoric road is also cited (Bakker 1976, pp. 77-8 suggests even two more side roads, dating to the Single Grave Culture).

Jager’s reconstruction of the road between Eext and dolmen Hunebed D8 is also mostly based on the position of cemeteries and barrows, the landscape and a few observations of a carttrack running parallel to these monuments. Part of the road was unknowingly recorded during excavations as a darke smudge in field drawings, running in N-S direction. Yet it was not identified as a road, so no photographic record or excavation drawings in section of the feature were made. No evidence for pavement was recorded either. Near Eext (Eextergrubben), the road was found in excavation next to tumuli as an iron pan infiltrated zone, in which podzolized wheel-tracks were found (1985, p. 191). The date for this road was based on burials / settlements along the route of this road. The oldest remains date to the Funnel Beaker culture and it continued to be in use until the Roman period (p. 192). Jager suggests a second road ran along the east flank of the Hondsrug based on the burial monuments (partly coinciding with the modern Oude Groningerweg). Cart tracks were also found in the banks of modern boundary ditches and also followed the alignment of barrows over 2 km. Furthermore a series of trenches were dug to record the road further, revealing a road of 1.5-1.7 m to 2 m wide. Of note is that the wheel ruts were found with a podsol profile developed in them, meaning that the road was abandoned for a while before it covered by decks of manure (*plaggenbemesting* or *essen*). In one case the ruts gradually disappeared by the ruts becoming shallower and in one instance the road partially coincedes with an old unpaved road of the 19th century. Jager carefully concludes that the origin of the road must be placed before 700-800 CE and possibly its use goes back to the late Bronze Age (1985, p. 196-7). The (hollow) road was a prehistoric road that was in use at least until the Roman period and there seems to have been a shift in Medieval times of the road, as the predecessor villages of Eext and Anlo shifted to their present position in Carolingian times (pp. 199-200). The covering up of the road by manure indicates the reuse of the road area for agriculture and ploughing may have destroyed large parts of the road as well.

The gauge of the carts could be retrieved from excavation, but they make a problematic dating tool. The measured gauges were 1.0-1.1 m wide. From other excavations, it is known that Neolithic gauge widths were some 1.5 m wide, while during the Bronze Age, 1.2 m set more or less the standard during later prehistory. In Medieval times, a 1.5 m width was common. In other words, the found gauge width are not clearly datable and there are naturally many unknowns and spatial variation in wheel gauges, which Jager discusses for the few examples from the Netherlands (i.e.: Kootwijk – Carolingian – 1 m and 1.4 m wide gauges; Wijster – Roman period settlement with early Medieval indications as well – 1.2 m wide; Odoorn – early Medieval – 1.2 m wide; Voorste Diep – road paved with stones 10th-11th century CE – 1.1-1.25 m gauge).

Jan Bakker’s study on roads of the Funnel Beaker Culture of the 1970s was updated in 2008. Fundamentally, his reconstruction relies on the position of barrows and cemeteries. Pieter Modderman already observed the linear distribution of barrows near Epe and already suggested that the barrows must be indicative for the presence of a road (Modderman 1955, p. 61). R.H.J. Klok carried out a survey on barrows on the Veluwe and also argued that the alignment indicative of a prehistoric road network (Klok 1982). The logic is that these barrows and cemeteries were placed, very much like Roman cemeteries, next to roads so passers by could see the buried locally important VIPs (Bakker 2008, p. 281). The landscape morphology played an important role, as roads avoided traffic-unfriendly terrain. In a comparison, Bakker mentions routes in Jutland, where preferred courses ran along the borderline of sandy and loamy soils, making them passable in winter and summer. Steep slopes were avoided. The use of two- and four-wheeled wagons may partially explain such requirements for the routes of roads and such wheeled wagons have been in use since 3400 cal BCE (Anthony 2007; Burmeister 2002; Bakker et al. 1999). In the Veluwe area and the northern Netherlands, the expansion of drift sands starting during the Bronze Age altered economic opportunities and must have altered routes to facilitate transhumance (Van Gijn & Waterbolk 1984). The routes Bakker reconstructed were based on lines of barrows near Epe, with a main line running southwest – northeast, which follows the main eastern slope of the Veluwe hill ridge and crossed five steep sided dry periglacial valleys, whilst avoiding bifurcations. The dates of these barrows vary from 2500 cal BCE and Bakker suggests this road may have been in use throughout the Bronze Age, Iron Age and possibly even up to the Roman period (2008, pp. 281-2). Three side branches are identified as well. *Raatakkers* are used as indicators of the orientation and possible dates of the routes, as these were situated next to the lines of the aligned barrows (see Bourgeois 2013, fig. 5.1 for the clearest overview).

The problem with reconstructing routes for roads using barrows is that the sepulchral and possibly sacral character of barrows possibly point to another reason for their linear distribution, rather than the following a route for traffic. Bakker refers to a study of barrows in England by Paul Garwood, aligned barrows in Wiltshire do not seem to indicate ongoing routes, as the lines do not connect settlements or other places. Rather, they were aligned with the Midwinter sunset and Midsummer sunrise. Bakker, however, is not convinced that the alignments of the Veluwe are sacral in nature, but that a practical use for traffic must have been part of the alignment (2008, p. 282). Quentin Bourgeois extensively studied the barrow alignments of the Veluwe and tackles problems around the interpretation of the barrow alignments (2013). The alignment between Epe and Niersen is almost oriented at the midwinter sunset, but it also is oriented at the smallest crossing point of a stream valley in the north, and possibly in the south as well (Bourgeois 2013, p. 189). Furthermore, he makes a diachronic distinction in the development of the barrows in the landscape, namely starting with the development of alignments in Late Neolithic A, while later barrows from the Late Neolithic B were erected more dispersed in the landscape (p. 194). Rather than focusing on the possible roads that the alignments might demarcate, he emphasize the cultural differentiation of the landscape namely that it was the heathland that was the area where the barrows were (mostly) erected, instead of focusing that roads played a major role as attractors for the construction of barrows. The fact that the alignments were situated along a road is of secondary importance: the barrows were constructed in an alignment to generate movement along its axis, thus creating a sequence of mortuary symbols (p. 192). And even if a road was situated along the barrows, a person walking from settlement to pasture or other settlement, the encountering of these barrows would have strengthened the sense of community and belonging to the social group to which the barrows belonged. In other words, the alignments were part of a sacred landscape, which may or may not have been part of a functioning road network. As roads have been evidenced next to urnfields, it is certainly possible that sepulchral monuments are indicative for the location of roads. Furthermore, as Niels Johannsen and Steffen Laursen (2010, p. 49) point out, the construction of funerary monuments within the landscape in itself is an act of (potentially) forming or reforming the trafficking routes through the landscape as the monuments become part of the topography. Without running the danger of reducing the discussion into a ‘Chicken or the Egg’ problem, the association of the alignments with movement and roads makes the use of barrows as proxies for movement through the landscape valid. And even if we take the lines of funerary monuments as sacral lines, these may well have formed attractors of routes of movement throughout the landscape, once established. The fact that *raatakkers* can sometimes be fit to these linear configurations, further underlines this possibility, assumed that these agricultural field play a less symbolic and more practical role than the barrows. Although not archaeologically established, the suggestion that historic roads coincide with these lines and thereby possibly hinting at long term continuity of use of these routes, also point in this direction. But even if it could be established that the barrow lines formed major traffic arteries in prehistory, that does in no way (which Bourgeois correctly argues) diminish the role of these lines in group formation, cohesion and dynamics of prehistoric societies.

It remains, however, an unknown what type of roads and transport these barrows may have indicated (if any) and, more importantly, what roads and routes existed throughout prehistory that were not demarcated by sepulchral monuments, for example in areas where barrows are not found. Obviously waterways played a role when present, but for overland transport during prehistory, to focus on the lines formed by (funerary) sites risks creating a bias towards alignments and so on while ignoring straight or more curving paths or roads that were not paralleled by barrows.

A study on the protohistoric and Roman roads and field systems in the civitas Menapiorum (roughly the area of the southern Netherlands, western Belgium and northern France) used a similar approach by combining historical, physical, and archaeological geographies to which an extensive analysis of aerial photographs was added as well as digital modelling techniques (Vermeulen & Antrop 2001). The main research topic of this study were linear features observed in maps, excavations, aerial photographs and so on. Frank Vermeulen noted a likely (though not attested) continuity of Roman roads from protohistoric roads (2001, p. 77). Once a route is chose, it simply continued to be used. The construction of roads, however, changed with the Roman conquest, with (local) sand and gravel being used for the embankment, consolidation and pavement of the roads and possibly the addition of wooden piles and timber frames to further strengthen the road (Vermeulen 2001, p. 80). An inventorization of excavations of roads showed little evidence for the continuity between the protohistoric and Roman road or route networks. In one instance an late Iron Age open air sanctuary which was no longer in use was crossed by a later Roman road (Vermeulen & Hageman 2001, pp. 98-9). In another instance a complex of road remains was found in the direct vicinity of a late Iron Age sanctuary (which continued to be in use during early Roman times). It is assumed that that the road remains date pre-Medieval and possibly pre-Roman as well, although the author caution against drawing conclusions on the exact chronology (idem, pp. 106-8). Of interest is an example of an excavation settlement showing a main road in SSE-NNW orientation with one smaller side road forking from the road towards a cemetery, demonstrating that cemeteries are not necessarily always indicative of the routes of (main) roads.

Modelling of the routes of Roman roads using Least Cost Path analysis in the same study, show significant differences between the modelled routes and the (reconstructed) routes of Roman roads. The latter followed more direct connection between settlements closer, whereas the modelled routes followed the more ‘efficient’ routes through the landscape (Wiedemann, Antrop & Vermeulen 2001). Among the explanations for this at the time was that the accuracy of the maps was quite low and that not all factors resulting in the ‘cost’ are known. The fact that these analyses take a bird-eye view to create the Least Cost Path, while the routes and roads were chosen from the ground also must have had an impact on the modelled results (idem, p. 90).

The assumption that Roman roads will have been founded upon and anchored in a protohistoric/prehistoric road network has merit and a recent study of Late Neolithic (Michelsberg culture) earthworks in Germany also seems to point to the long continuity of routes. These sites consisted of ditches and palisades, although they did not necessarily enclosed an area entirely (e.g. Koslar; Schyle et al. 2015, pp. 360-1). One earthwork found near Soest is situated directly on a historical “*Hellweg*”, which were medieval long distance east-west routes. Some 40 km further east, near Salzkotten-Oberntudorf, two of these medieval roads came together, and here too a Late Neolithic earthwork was situated. (Knoche & Schyle 2015, pp. 367-8, 371). Further research on prehistoric sites in Germany seems to point to the continued use of long distance routes, which simply followed the easiest routes through the landscape and many earthworks are concentration at transition or crossing zones in the landscape, such as fords, passes, edges of terrain or forks in the road. It might be the case that these prehistoric routes were mainly used for transhumance, connecting seasonal pastures. Benedikt Knoche and Daniel Schyle interpret the function of the earthworks along these routes as social anchoring points for societies that were spread across the landscape and thus functioned to strengthen the group cohesion by collectively constructing these earthworks and perhaps using these places for seasonal religious feasts or rituals (2015, p. 371 – although they also emphasize the relative rarity of these types of sites). These earthworks may therefore point to mobility and transport during Neolithic times, and quite possibly these earthworks played a role in seasonal activities, such as religious feasts, exchange of goods or the collection of herds of livestock (Schyle et al. 2015, p. 364).

The largest of these Neolithic earthworks is situated between Stetternich and Jülich, next to a stretch of the famous road between Boulogne-sur-Mer (Gesoriacum) and Köln (Colonia Agrippinensis), also known by the (ahistorical) name *via Belgica*. The Roman road followed a straight line coinciding with the Kölner Landstraße, oriented east-northeast to west-southwest (Hagen 1926, pp. 12-15; Andrikopoulou-Strack 2008, pp. 17-29). Immediately to the north of this straight line aerial photography revealed the remains of a Late Neolithic earthwork that was later studied using geophysics, survey and excavations. This earthwork consisted of two parallel ditches encircling an area of ca. 55 ha, or 840 m in diameter. The Roman road seems to cut through the southern part of the enclosure. Although there is no evidence that the Roman road is built (directly) upon a prehistoric path or road, the suggestion that this place was already an important node for transport in Neolithic times might indicate that the Roman road followed routes that were already in existence. Jülich (named Juliacum cf. *It. Ant.* 375.8; 378.7; TP 1A5.593) was an important node in Roman times with a vicus (Hagen 1926, p. 13).  The problem with this interpretation, however, is the large gap in time between the possile date of this earthwork (i.e. 4th millenium BCE; Schyle et al. 2015, p. 362) and the construction of the Roman road. At the same time, this analysis forms a welcome contribution to the reconstruction of prehistoric routes, especially as it is not focused on the location of funerary monuments.

In the research area, two possible instances of protohistoric roads are excavated (see Nijmegen-Noord 1 and 2 below). Here two instances of elongated gravel concentrations have been found, one instance over a length of 150 m and the other 180 m. The width is no longer determinable, although compacted clay of the concentration continues over a width of 10 m. In the south, the width of the concentration may have been 20 m. Heavy ploughing may have disturbed and spread the concentration significantly. Of interest is that this concentration is dates with a mix of find materials, which may be indicative of a palimpsest with later roads (Broeke 2013, pp. 37-40). Although the road is dated to the early Iron Age in the north and a late Bronze Age – early Iron Age date in the south (Daniël 2015, p. 19), these concentrations align and there are indications for continued use in later times, possibly even the Roman period. A early to middle Roman cemetery was excavated some 150 m west of the southern concentration (Daniël 2015, pp. 19-20). The general orientation of these two observations is on an north-south axis, roughly connecting Elst with Nijmegen. To reconstruct this as a possible prehistoric route connecting settlements on the Waal with settlements in the centre of the Betuwe around Elst is attractive, especially if a continuity of use into the Roman period may be assumed: both Nijmegen and Elst yield major Roman monumental sites. Some 500 m west of the northern gravel concentration, a settlement was excavated at Oosterhout, which had a north-south orientation, oriented on a straight line between the Roman city of Ulpia Noviomagus and the Gallo-Roman temple of Elst. Its earliest phase may go back to the Bronze Age, but the it certainly existed during the 3rd century BCE (Van den Broeke 2002, pp. 12-8; Willems & Van Enckevort 2009, pp. 90-2). During the 1st to 3rd century CE, the settlement flourished and had a size of 4.5-6 ha with probably five farms existing simultaneously. No road is found in the excavations of the settlement, but the north-south orientation and the continuity of the settlement from the Iron Age to Roman period are of interest. As the possible road found 500 m east is possibly of earlier date, contemporaneous existence at some point could be envisioned as well. Quite possibly, Nijmegen-Noord 1 and 2 were part of a road bypassing the settlement at Oosterhout. It may even be conceivable, given the orientation of the settlement, that a second road following a north-south axis was situated close to this settlement, but which was not preserved or detected. Although these proposals at the moment represent informed speculation and more research along the axis between Elst and Nijmegen is required, the continued use of the possible road and habitation of the settlement through the Iron Age into the Roman period, forms a possible archaeologically substantiated example where protohistoric routes formed the basis for Roman roads.


## What is a road?

So far the words road, route, path, trackway, causeway, etc. have all been used in this text, relying on the intuitive or common interpretation of the reader. Although this has (hopefully) not posed major problems for the understanding of the reader so far, it is necessary to clarify and define all these terms. Not only does this serve to facilitate clarity in the further discussion on Roman roads, but also to prelude on the further exploration what the functions and impact, both intended and unintended, of roads are.
