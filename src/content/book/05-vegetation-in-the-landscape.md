# Vegetation in the Landscape

The study of botanical remains can give an insight in vegetation and landuse of this area. A process of siltation of peatbogs in the central Rhine river area took place during the first centuries CE, leaving massive claydecks on top of the peat. This process was in part the result of deforestation along the Rhine valley further upstream in Germany (Vos et al. 2018, p. 66). A similar result of deforestation is also visible in the Löss sediments towards the Meuse river valley in the south of Limburg during the same period. The erosion of Löss in Germany caused by deforestation and agricultural practices of the Late Bronze Age, caused the lowlands along the Rhine in the east of Gelderland and the west of Nordrhein-Westfalen to be transformed. By the end of the Late Iron Age this area was no longer an area of streamridges and floodplains, but rather turned into a flatland covered with clay (Bork et al. 2003; Van Beek, Van den Broeke & Willemse in Smit et al. 2022, p. 115). This erosion / sedimentation process also affected and changed the courses of major rivers, such as the Waal and Nederrijn.

For the Betuwe and the Dutch River Area, the streamridges (former riverbeds together with former levees) were the focal points of both settlement and agricultural exploitation, as these were the least likely to flood in a river landscape. During the Iron Age, these were largely deforested, presumably by human intervention (Kooistra 2009). During the Roman period, agriculture was focused on streamridges in the Betuwe and the Dutch River Area, both livestock rearing and (grain) cultivation with little evidence of forests on these ridges (Lange 1990; Kooistra 1996; Groot & Kooistra 2009). Flood basins are the lowest areas, which makes them more susceptible to flooding and also less suitable for cultivation. Still, as pastures or meadows for livestock, they were suitable and were likewise used during Roman times. Levees were situated next to an active river and also are susceptible to flooding. Groot and Kooistra (2009) suspect these were similarly exploited as streamridges, but perhaps less intensily used and inhabited. Woodland may have existed here during Roman times and used for (communal) exploitation. Modelling based on the Roman landscape, agricultural potential and settlement patterns have indicated that agricultural surplus production was possible in the Dutch River Area and it is quite possible that the roads were (part of) the transport arteries to bring goods from the rural areas to central markets (Groot et al. 2009). Whether these markets were military *vici* or cities remains an open question.

On the Veluwe, evidence from the excavations at the Roman temporary camp at Ermelo yielded slightly conflicting evidence, with the macrobotanical suggesting that oaktrees must have grown extensively here during Roman times, with beeches, ashes, and alders growing more in the open humid areas (Kooistra in Hulst 2007, pp. 55-9). The pollen evidence indicates an open woodland area here with heath and grasses as undergrowth in pre-Roman times with some indications of agricultural cultivation (pollen of grain / cerealia) and alder which underlines the open character of the woodland (Groenman-van Waateringe in Hulst 2007, pp. 61-9). There is a trend visible in the pollen of an increase of heath by a factor of 3-4 times, with a decline in pollen from trees, suggesting a growth of the heath area with a reduction of forest. During the Roman period, the area consisted mostly of heath with groups of trees and solitary trees forming the only wood, with no indication for agricultural cultivation or livestock rearing. The only exception may be sheep rearing as this goes hand in hand with the increasing area of heath. Farther North near Zwolle, a combined approach of micro- and macrobotanical research and geomorphological studies allowed for the reconstruction for a wetland forest (Kooistra et al. 2006). Here, although indications for human influence could be detected, overall there was a limited human impact on the development of the landscape from the Iron Age to Late Roman times.

For the eastern part of the research area, reconstructions of vegetation are based mostly of four pollen samples and analyses taken in the wider vicinity of Nijmegen, namely at Horssen – Laagveld in the Land van Maas en Waal area 16 km to the west, Nijmegen – Dukenburg 5 km to the southwest of the city centre of Oppidum Batavadorum, Haps - de Hoef to the south-west of Cuijk on the west bank of the Meuse, Gennep – Kleefsche Beek on the east bank of the Meuse, and near Wyler, to the east Ulpia Noviomagus and the legionary fortress of the Hunerberg  (These studies go back in no small extent to the work by D. Teunissen from the 1970s to 1990s; This part is a summary of the chapter by Kalis et al. 2008, pp. 29-48).

At Horssen-Laagveld there existed an area of floodplain forests was a floodplain between Molenhoek and Donken Horssen. C14 dates are somewhat problematic in this sample, but a vegetation reconstruction sequence can nevertheless be made. From the Iron Age onwards a transformation of the landscape can be observed, with the disappearance of (the palynological signature characteristic for) forests and the rise of high values for grasspollen. Areas were turned from forests into agricultural fields and pastures, demonstrating heavy Iron Age landuse. By the end of the 1st century BCE, the area seems to be under heavy agricultural use and there is no significant variation with the Imperial period. During Late Roman times, there is reforestation happening, showing the abandonment of agricultural fields and pastures (Kalis et al. 2008, pp. 37-39).

Closer to Nijmegen, at Dukenburg (Kalis et al. 2008, pp. 39-40) the landscape was part of a trench of the morain here and was covered with starweed-oak-hornbeam forest and oak-beech forest on the moraine and coversands. Here too, there are indicators for intense agricultural activity during early Iron Age, with a peak visible in the 4th century BCE. Even in the late Iron Age, this area was probably under intense agricultural exploitation. There is a notable indication for settlement indicators in the pollen (especially grain), although a dip in these during the 2nd half of the 1st century CE could possibly be related to the Batavian Revolt (69-70 CE). Afterwards there is a quick restore in vegetation. During the 3rd century CE (at Dukenburg from 200 CE), there is an increase in pioneering plants, indicative of the desertion of pastures and agricultural fields and there are signs of reforestation. This is possibly an effect of the wars and political unrest of the 3rd century.

Further south, on the west bank of the Meuse, another sample was taken at Haps de Hoef, which was situated at 1 km of a coversand ridge. The impact on the landscape and vegetation by human intervention seems lower, as the soils perhaps did not have the same opportunities for agriculture. In the sample (400-200 BCE) there are signs for alder-oak forest and during the IA specifically there are signs of an increase in linden trees. No data for the Roman period are available for this sample (Kalis et al. 2008, pp. 40-1).

Across the Meuse, on its east bank between Gennep and Boxmeer, at Kleefsche Beek, the pollen tell a different story. This area is located on the edge of hardwood forest of oak-hornbeam and eight C14 dates could be obtained. The pollen shows an increase in the values for pollen related to grassland and open land from the Iron Age (here 500 BCE) onwards, while pollen related to forested area is dwindling, which is caused by increased livestock rearing and agriculture. The increase in landuse continues into the late Iron Age with little difference visible for the Roman phase. This means that the landscape was probably already under heavy cultivation when the Romans arrived in this area in the later 1st century BCE. The end of Roman occupation is visible by the dwindling of pollen counts indicative for settlement and agriculture (grain, sorrel, ribwort plantain, heath, bracken, grasses) to a level as low as during Neolithic times. This is visible from the 2nd half of the 3rd to 5th century CE, when agricultural fields and pastures were being given up and forests re-emerged. By the 5th century, the area was reforested and agricultural activity becomes visible only by the 7th century (Kalis et al. 2008, pp. 33-7).

To the east of Nijmegen, pollen samples were analyses at Wyler, which is situated in a long moorland area, immediately south of the floodplain of the Rhine on the foot of the moraine here. Here, there is a high value for pine trees (30 % higher than the other samples discussed here (Kalis et al. 2008, pp. 41-2)). The coversands here have little nutrients, which might explain the presence of pine trees, as these thrive better on soils with poor nutrient levels. There is no signal in the pollen for human intervention during the Bronze Age here, but during the Iron Age, the trees must have been cut. This must have had an effect on the watersupply (less trees to ‘store’ the water), with more water flowing down from the highground to the lowgrounds. Here streams may have come into being, as the development of willows indicates. During the late Iron Age, the high level of birch pollen is changed for high levels in oak pollen (this is also visible at Dukenburg). The sample stops around the beginning of the 1st century CE. Despite the fact that environmental conditions were unfavourable for agriculture due to the coversands, there is a high signal for landscape exploitation through agriculture, which lasted at least until the Romans came.

These samples show local differences, but overall they show a development in landuse in the area around Nijmegen, starting from the middle Bronze Age. Landuse changed and started to include more livestock-rearing and new farm/house types, with an increase of grassland, pastures and meadows visible into the Iron Age. During the 4th century BCE, a peak of agricultural cultivation and livestock rearing is visible on the coversand areas and wetlands, but mostly in the floodplains, levees, and streamridges of the Rhine lowlands. Around 200 BCE, there is a peak in pioneering plants, indicating a shift in landuse. Woodland became dominated by oak, replacing the dominance of birches. Landuse seems to have been optimized for demographic needs during the late Iron Age and the Romans find an agriculturally well developed area at the end of the 1st century BCE. Historical evens are not clearly visible in the pollen evidence (e.g. invasion of Romans, Batavians, etc.). Minor changes are visible, such as shifts to new agricultural practices (Lössarea – switch towards villa rustica surplus farming), but the most dramatic shift is visible during the Late Roman period (from the 3rd century CE onwards) with clear signs of reforestation indicating the desertion of agricultural fields, pastures, and meadows. From Xanten, the evidence of pollen stems from inside the city proper and, combined with macrobotanical remains and bone material, they show a similar pattern for the Iron Age and Roman period as Nijmegen, with lots of grassland around Xanten existing up to the 4th century CE, when clear signs of reforestation and a decline of openland indicators are visible.
