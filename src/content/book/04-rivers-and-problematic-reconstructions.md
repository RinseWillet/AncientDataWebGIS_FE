# Rivers and Problematic Reconstructions

Even more alterations to the landscape can be found in the lower floodplain areas of the rivers Meuse, Rhine, and Waal, where the courses of these rivers have altered significantly since ancient times. Alterations are continuously made to cope with new realities in water management (for example the recent *Ruimte voor de Rivieren* project was designed to cope with high water levels in the rivers in the Netherlands to prevent (catastrophic and uncontrolled) flooding. Alterations were made to the courses of the rivers in historic times as well, such as the construction of the Pannerdensch Kanaal im 1706 or Bijlandsch Kanaal in 1773-6 which both cut off old maeanders. Yet for the situation two millenia ago we must rely on paleogeographic reconstructions. In this period natural development of the river courses and possibly human intervention both played a role in determining the course of the major rivers. For the Netherlands, the reconstruction of the riverfloodplains and the landscape in pre- and protohistoric times has in the last two decades received a few major contributions. Kim Cohen and Esther Stouthamer (2012) reconstructed the channel and floodplain sequences through the collection of thousands of coring surveys. Building on this reconstruction, Harm Jan Pierik (2017) made a reconstruction of the landscape in the central Netherlands during the first millenium CE. The *Atlas of the Holocene Netherlands* by Peter Vos, Michiel van der Meulen, Henk Weerts and Jos Bazelmans (2020) also provides a small scale reconstruction of the Netherlands as a whole. The advantage of these reconstructions is that they are all made publicly available as digital datasets, usuable in any modern Geographic Information System.

The problem with these reconstructions is that they correspond on the small scale, but on the large scale, there are problematic differences that directely affect the places where roads could have been situated in the Roman period. An example of this is the area of Nijmegen. When comparing the reconstructions mentioned for Nijmegen in 100 CE, there are some striking differences, with the course of the Waal in the *Atlas of the Holocene* differing fundamentally in location and number of branches with the reconstruction by Pierik. The recent landscape reconstruction for the same area by Eckhart Heunks (2023) shows the course of the Waal again being markedly different (compare Figures 3 to 6). As this latter reconstruction incorporates and evaluates the most and latest corings, subsurface observations and C14 dates for the this location (and as his work was reviewed by Kim Cohen), we may assume that for Nijmegen, Heunks’ reconstruction is the most reliable. Yet it shows a problem with landscape reconstructions for the Netherlands, which is that the reconstructions that exist generally work well for the small scale, viewing the Rhine-Meuse delta as a whole or per province, but for the large scale / local level, there are still many unknowns and conflicting visions, which will have an affect on reconstructions of routes of ancient roads. A solution to this problem could be an iterative development cycle of the landscape reconstruction maps, where local investigations could be added to result in a yearly updated version for the Netherlands as a whole. A project akin to the Open Street Map project, where the data would be made available as open source (which is true for all reconstructions mentioned via platforms like Dans Easy of the KNAW), there would be necessary a process by which new data are added and reviewed to the main dataset. As such a dataset is lacking at the moment, this study will mostly use the landscape reconstructions by Cohen, Stouthamer and Pierik, with additions of local reconstructions.

When looking at the German part of the research area, there is relative stability visible, with the one exception being the active channelbelt near Kleve becoming inactive during the Roman period (compare Figure 7 to Figure 9). The Rhine in this area was relatively stable, which must have been caused by hydrological infrastructure and specific interventions to protect areas and prevent maeanders from changing (Mirschenz & Gerlach 2022 pp. 129-30). For the Dutch area, there are clear shifts near Nijmegen in the Waal, as well as massive shifts in the Eastern Betuwe near Arnhem and Wijk bij Duurstede in the course of the Rhine. Another major shift is visible in the course of the Waal near Tiel. A connection existed between the Waal and Rhine via Tiel in 100 CE, with the Waal course continuing further west from Tiel. Only by 1750 14C BP, the Waal seems to follow the course from Tiel towards the south-west and connecting to the Meuse, resembling more the modern course of the singular riverstream that the Waal has become (Jansma et al. 2017; Lanen et al. 2018; Van der Heijden & Verhelt 2020). This must have had an impact on the routes that shipping could have travelled, with traffic coming from the Waal more easily reaching the Rhine to the north in the first centuries of Roman occupation. The connection between the Meuse and Waal is a matter of ongoing discussion (Van der Heijden & Verhelst 2020, p. 21). An inventory of ships of the Roman period in Gelderland clearly shows a concentration near Tiel and near the Meuse between Alphen towards Rossum, where junctions of waterways existed or supposed to have existed (Van der Heijden 2020; see also Van Hemert 2010, pp. 51-68 for a study of Kessel-Lith, Rossum, and Alem as an important traffic junction node for (heavy bulk) traffic coming from south upstream the Meuse). The course of the Meuse in the west of the research area also changed significantly, consolidating into a singular stream. More upstream, the Meuse is relatively stable. Furthermore, during the later Iron Age and the 1st century CE, new watercourses emerged in the central Rhine river area in the Netherlands, which led to more water flowing towards the Meuse and to less water running via the Oude Rijn towards the coast.

The Rhine river in Roman times formed a border and traffic land and it ran (from west to east) along the rivers Oude Rijn, Kromme Rijn, and Nederrijn in the Netherlands, and from Bijland the Rhine river, where it also followed old riverarms, like Kermisdahl and Wetering near Kleve or the area of Xanten where it roughly followed the track of the Pistley or Pisstsche Ley (Mirschenz & Gerlach 2022, p. 130). A large part of the research area consists of floodplains of the large rivers already mentioned, plus a number of tributary rivers and streams, such as the Niers or the Linge, and residual channels. In these plains, areas with riverdunes exist along the Meuse (e.g. Wijchen, Gennep etc.) and Rhine (e.g. Duiven).
