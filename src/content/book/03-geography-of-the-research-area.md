# The Geography of the Research Area

The research area was situated in the northern part of the Roman Empire on the European continent, which housed two cities (namely Nijmegen, or Ulpia Noviomagus, and Xanten, or Colonia Ulpia Traiana - Figure 1). Each city also had strong ties to the Roman army, as they both had a legionary garrison, albeit not continuously or simultaneously. This area was part of the northern frontier zone: several forts (typically named *castella*) are attested or assumed in this region, all directly connected to the major rivers flowing through this area, which are the Rhine, Waal, and Meuse. In the Dutch area, these rivers run east to west, with the Meuse coming from the south and turning westward near Cuijk, Mook and Katwijk, while the Rhine curves from the south and meanders towards the west roughly from the area near Xanten.

![Map of the research area outline](fig01-research-area-overview.jpg "Figure 1 Outline of the research area in red with the cities, legionary fortresses and forts of the Roman period plotted.")

The area is also characterized by several highgrounds. Most notably in the Netherlands, these are the area of the Utrecht Hill Ridge (Utrechtse Heuvelrug) and Veluwe, both of which consist of extensive push moraine complexes. The Lower Rhine forms the southern border from these two areas. On the Dutch-German border, the Lower Rhine Heights (Niederrheinischen Höhenzug) is another highground, which consists of terminal moraines (Klostermann 2008, pp. 24-26). All these moraines are formed during the penultimate glacial period (Saalian glaciation – 400-300,000 – 126,000 years ago). The Lower Rhine Heights dominate the eastern part of the research area. It is bordered to the north by the Eltener Höhen ridge (Bergherbos), while the Rhine Valley (Lower Rhine Plain) borders the north and east, while to the south and west, the Niers river valley forms its edge. Several modern cities and towns are located here, including Nijmegen, Kleve, Kalkar and Xanten on its northern side, with Goch and Uedem and a little further Kevelaer and Geldern are situated to the south. Two ridges that shape a W dominate this terrain in the west, namely the Zeven Heuvelen ridge running from Nijmegen to Mook and curving around Groesbeek towards Kranenburg and the Kranenburger Höhenrand curving from the south of Kranenburg around Frasselt and Schottheide towards Nütterden and Kleve. Another ridge, the Pfalzdorfer Höhenrand, is present further east and runs from Uedem to Kalkar, while between Uedem and Xanten, the highground is formed by the Balbecker Höhenrücken, Uedemer Hochwald, Tüschenwald and the Sonsbecker Schweiz. Xanten itself is situated to the north-west of Hees, another ridge, and further south the Bönninghardt forms another outlier of the Lower Rhine Heights (The Schaephuser Höhenzug is also part of the Lower Rhine Heights, but lies outside the research area).

The highgrounds have altered from ancient times by erosion and deforestation and changes made to the terrain by modern infrastructural works, such as railroads, and urban expansion. A major historical alteration to the landscape can be found in the area of Goch, Kleve and Kalkar. In this area new settlement was initiated during the 18th century CE, when inhabitants (mainly protestants, Mennonites and Huguenots, the *kurpfälzische Auswanderer*) from the Pfalz area in south-western Germany fled poverty and religious unrests to find a new home in Nordrhein Westfalen. Not only did this resettlement impacted local dialects (the so-called *Pfälzische Sprachinseldialekt*) but it altered the landscape as well. Initially the area of the Gocher Heide was settled to found Pfalzdorf and the area turned into agricultural land. In 1820, the forests of the Kalkarwald, Eichenwald, and Buchenwald (an area of nearly 17 km2) were cut down to found the settlements of Louisendorf and Neulouisendorf (compare Figure 1 and Figure 2). Large tracts of land were turned into agricultural plots and field to sustain these settlements. This undoubtedly has led to erosion on the northern edge of the Lower Rhine Heights, but a study has not been carried out and parts of the forested areas on the highground edge still remain (sadly, the complete study of soil formation processes and erosion throughout Germany (Bork et al. 2003) does not deal with the Lower Rhine Heights area).

The highground area of the Veluwe and around Nijmegen saw the opposite process happening during the latter half of the 19th century. These terrains were largely uncultivated and especially the Veluwe was dealing with large swaths of areas where no soils could be formed as the sands were under direct aeolic influence, forming sand dune areas. Also the Nederrijkswald south of Nijmegen was around 1830 only heath and shrub. Both commercial (Heidemij – 1888) and governmental agencies (Staatsbosbeheer -  1899) started to exploit these uncultivated lands through forestry as a land reclamation program. In these areas there was an increase in amount of forested area and erosion processes slowed.
