# Land Use Before the Romans: Raatakkers

There are many indirect indicators of landuse for the Iron Age and Roman periods, such as the location and density of settlement sites or the paleobotanical or paleozoological remains found at such sites. But one of the clearest indicators of agricultural landuse in pre-Roman times are prehistoric field systems, also known by the problematic term “Celtic Fields” (Yates 2007; Arnoldussen 2018; Arnold 2017). As the “Celts” have little to do with the construction of these fields, the (Dutch) term *raatakker* is used here. *Raatakkers*, although not a Roman feature, can be useful indicators for later roads as their vastness (some can cover dozens of hectares) can be useful to spot post-prehistoric roads in the landscape. In other words, when *raatakkers* are found it is possible to check whether paths or roads carve through them, which provide a tentative terminus post quem. Furthermore they represent direct evidence of agricultural exploitation and are therefore proxies for settlements or demographic density and thereby *raatakkers* are also broadly indicative for the presence of pre-Roman transitroutes.

*Raatakkers* are known from the Low Countries, Germany, southern Scandinavia, and England and consist of plots of land of 20-40 m wide that are marked by a small wall or ridge. These walls may have begun as small fences against which the accumulation of material removed from the fields resulted in small ridges (Arnoldussen & van der Linden 2017; Arnoldussen 2018, p. 313). Examples have been excavated at Zeijen, Someren, Wekerom and Westeinde reveal that these systems developed from the Middle Bronze Age, Iron Age and some continued to have been used well into the Roman period (up to the 2nd century CE), using Optically Stimulated Luminescence dating and Accelerator Mass Spectrometry to obtain radiocarbon dates, and dating based on smallfinds (Arnoldussen 2018, p. 319). Furthermore, (the few) palynological studies of *raatakkers* show shifts in use of these fields, but much remains unclear on the use-cycles.

Although ridges are often very low and barely visible on the ground with eyesight alone, the vastness of these systems often makes them all the more visible from the air (e.g. Brongers 1976; Figure 12-Figure 16 ). Field conditions often have a significant influence on the visibility of archaeological features. Crop marks (discolouration of or grow height differences in crops caused by subsurface features), snow marks (variation in melting or snow coverage caused by differences in thermal characteristics by features in the subsurface), or soil marks (discolouration of the soil caused by different soil characteristics of subsurface features) are visible in aerial photography only when certain conditions are met. For example, soil marks are only visible when the bare soil is visible for instance in ploughed fields. Crop marks tend to be visible when a crop is under stress, for instance in unusually dry or wet seasons. Snow marks obviously are only visible when there are snowy conditions and when the temperature is oscillating around melting point (Lodewijckx & Peregrin 2011). During the last two decades, Light/Laser Detection and Ranging (LIDAR) has been applied in archaeology, which proved to be a major addition to the toolbox for archaeological feature detection (Rowlands & Sarris 2007; Verschoof-van der Vaart 2022; Kaptijn et al. 2023). LIDAR can survey the surface of a terrain in high resolution and can scan through forest canopies. With LIDAR it is possible to filter out both vegetation and buildings to create a digital terrain model of the landscape. Government agencies have been investing in creating nation covering surveys of LIDAR data. For the Netherlands, the data are made available to the public as the *Actueel Hoogte Bestand* (AHN3), while for Germany the data is available as the *Digitales Geländemodell* (DGM1). These two datasets are fairly comparable, making cross border analysis possible. The availability of these data to the public and the ability to ‘look under the trees’ and to detect minute variations in height has contributed greatly to the detection of *raatakkers* (for Germany, see Hesse 2010; Arnold 2011) and other archaeological features, including Roman roads (Berkel & Obladen-Kauder 2016). Major inventories of *raatakkers* using both aerial photography as LIDAR data have published for both the Netherlands as Germany (Bruns 2016; Arnold 2017; Leenders & Arnoldussen 2023) and are increasingly being made abailable through online repositories (https://www.celticfields.net/ and https://celtic-fields.com/).

In the researcharea there is a clear focus of (possible) *raatakker* sightings on the edges of the push-moraines of the Veluwe and the Reichswald and Hochwald on the Lower Rhine Heights. Outside these area’s there are few sightings, which may be caused by the relative undamaged character of these highgrounds – they still are for a large part natural preservation reserves with little to no modern agriculture, whereas outside these areas, the rivers may have eroded any prehistoric field systems or modern deep ploughing and levelling of fields may have simple removed them from visibility. In other words, it cannot be excluded, and archaeological evidence of settlements and palynological evidence seems to concur, that pre-Roman agriculture took place on an exhaustive scale outside these areas too. Still, the fact that along the edge of the Reichswald near Kleve tumuli and raatakkers are found in close proximity of an archaeologically confirmed stretch of Roman road, possibly indicates that prehistoric routes followed the edges of highgrounds as well. Before drawing such conclusions it is necessary to review the knowledge on prehistoric roads and routes in the Low Countries and Nordrhein Westfalen.
